The Corporate-to-Business Transition Checklist

For senior professionals thinking seriously about leaving to build something of their own.

You've spent years mastering how large organisations work. Business rewards a different set of muscles. This checklist walks you through what to honestly assess, what to put in place before you move, and the things people most often skip, then later regret skipping.

Work through it slowly. If an item makes you uncomfortable, that's usually the one worth sitting with.

Part 1

Assess, before anything else

Money and Runway

  • I know my true monthly cost of living, not the number I assume.

  • I have, or have a plan for, 12 to 18 months of personal runway that does not depend on the business succeeding quickly.

  • My spouse or family knows the numbers and is aligned, not just informed.

The Idea Itself

  • I can state, in one sentence, the specific problem I solve and for whom.

  • I know who pays for that solution today, and roughly what they pay.

  • At least three people outside my head have said "I'd buy that," and ideally, "here's my money."

Yourself, Honestly

  • I've separated the parts of my success that were me from the parts that were my company's brand, budget, and network.

  • I know which skills I'll have to build that a title used to give me for free: selling, pricing, chasing invoices, doing my own admin.

  • I know who I am without the job title, because clients won't be impressed by it.

Part 2

Prepare, put these in place first

Proof Before You Leap

  • I've validated real demand: a paying pilot, a signed letter of intent, or a waitlist, not just enthusiasm.

  • I've mapped my first ten likely clients by name, not by category.

  • I've had honest conversations with people who left corporate before me, including ones who struggled.

The Mechanics

  • I've chosen a business structure and understand the tax and liability basics.

  • I have a clear offer, a clear price, and a reason the price is what it is.

  • I have the essentials: a way to invoice, a way to get paid, a simple contract, a basic online presence.

The Transition Itself

  • I know how I'm leaving: timing, notice, and what I can and can't take, such as contacts, IP, and non-competes.

  • I've protected key relationships so bridges stay intact.

  • I have a start date, not just an intention.

Part 3

Never skip

These are the ones that quietly sink capable people.

Don't quit on hope alone. Validated demand comes before the resignation letter, not after.

Don't underprice to feel safe. Cheap doesn't win senior clients; it signals you don't believe in the value.

Don't underestimate the identity shift. Going from "SVP at a known company" to "founder nobody's heard of yet" is an emotional adjustment, not just a career one.

Don't isolate. Line up advisors, peers, or a community before you leave the built-in one you're walking away from.

Don't confuse revenue with cash flow. A profitable business can still run out of money. Watch what's actually in the account.

Don't skip the family conversation twice. Align at the start, and re-align when it gets hard, because it will.

If this raised more questions than it answered, that's the point. The transition is rarely about capability. You have that. It's about preparation, timing, and honesty with yourself.
