How Much of Your Retirement Savings Should Go Into a Business?

A clear framework for deciding how much of your gratuity, pension lump sum, or savings you can safely put into a business, and how much you should protect.

By Dr. Omowunmi Olalere, Founder & CEO, Numero Group

There is a number most advisors will not give you, because saying it out loud feels like taking on liability. So instead of the number, I will give you the framework, which is more useful anyway, since your number was never going to be the same as mine.

When a senior professional retires, a lump sum tends to arrive that is larger than any single amount they have held before. A gratuity, a pension commutation, the reward for years of discipline. And almost at once, the question follows: how much of this should go into a business?

The wrong way to answer is to reach for a percentage you read somewhere. The better way is to build the answer outward from your own life.

Start with what must never be touched

Before you work out what to invest, work out what is not investable at all. Divide your capital into three layers, and take them in order.

Layer one: your security floor

This is the money that funds the life you have already promised yourself: living costs, medical cover, the dependents you support, and the buffer that lets you absorb an emergency without borrowing. As a rough guide, this layer should cover at least three to five years of your essential expenses, held somewhere safe and within reach. Not in a business. Not in an illiquid asset. Not lent to a relative's venture.

Treat this floor as untouchable. It is the very thing that lets you take any risk at all.

Layer two: your risk capital

Only what sits above the security floor is available to deploy, and that is your true risk capital. The honest test for it is simple. Ask yourself: if this entire amount vanished, would my standard of living change? If the answer is yes, the figure is too high, so bring it down until the answer becomes no.

For many retirees, this settles somewhere between 10 and 25 percent of total liquid capital for a first venture. Treat that as a range to reason from, not a rule to obey. If your floor is unusually well covered, say by a pension or rental income, your risk capital can be larger. If the floor is thin, it should be smaller, however attractive the opportunity looks.

Layer three: reserve for the business itself

Here is the mistake that quietly ruins otherwise good ventures. People pour their entire risk capital into launching, and keep nothing back for surviving.

A new business almost always takes longer to reach profit than the plan promises. So whatever you set aside as risk capital, split it in two. Commit part of it to the launch, and hold the rest as an operating reserve, enough to carry the business through the slow early months without forcing you back to your security floor. A useful discipline is to commit no more than two-thirds of your risk capital at the outset, and keep the remainder in reserve.

Why the ceiling matters more than the opportunity

Experienced professionals tend to underestimate just how differently business capital behaves compared with the capital they managed during a career.

In a salaried life, income arrived on a schedule and losses belonged to someone else. In business, income is irregular and every loss is yours. Money you put in is not sitting quietly; it is exposed every single day to decisions, markets, and people you do not fully control. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is precisely the reason to cap it.

The professionals who protect their retirement are seldom the ones who found the best opportunity. They are the ones who fixed their ceiling before the opportunity ever appeared, so that when an exciting deal did arrive, and one always does, they had already settled with themselves how far they were willing to go.

What never to do with a pension or lump sum

  • Never deploy your security floor, however certain the return appears. Certainty is a feeling, not a fact.
  • Never commit a lump sum on the day it arrives. Give yourself a deliberate waiting period. The very pressure you feel to "put it to work" is what tends to produce poor decisions.
  • Never fund someone else's business from your retirement capital on the strength of the relationship alone. If it cannot survive being treated as a real investment, with terms, structure, and an exit, then it is a gift, and you should size it as one.
  • Never borrow against your pension to enlarge what you can invest. Leverage magnifies the one outcome you genuinely cannot afford.

The real question underneath

"How much should I invest?" is usually standing in for a harder question: how much certainty do I need before I feel safe?

Answer that one honestly and the number tends to reveal itself. The arithmetic is not the hard part. The discipline is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a single safe percentage of my savings to invest?

No honest advisor can give you one, because it depends entirely on your security floor. The reliable rule is the test itself: put in only capital whose total loss would not change how you live. For many first ventures that lands between 10 and 25 percent of liquid capital, but the test matters far more than the range.

Should I invest my gratuity as soon as I receive it?

No. A deliberate waiting period is one of the most protective decisions you can make. The urge to put a lump sum to work the moment it arrives is exactly what leads to over-committed, under-researched choices.

Can I invest more if the opportunity looks unusually strong?

Set your ceiling before you evaluate any particular opportunity. If a strong one tempts you past your security floor, the issue is not that your ceiling is too low. It is that the opportunity is asking you to risk money you cannot afford to lose.
