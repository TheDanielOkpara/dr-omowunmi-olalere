Your Professional Network Is Your First Business Asset

After decades in corporate Nigeria, your network may be worth more than your savings, provided you draw on it the right way. Here is how to do that with integrity.

By Dr. Omowunmi Olalere, Founder & CEO, Numero Group

After decades in corporate Nigeria, the most valuable thing you bring to a new business may not be your savings at all. It may be the network you spent a whole career building, as long as you know how to draw on it without wearing it down.

Most first-time founders begin as strangers. They have no reputation, no relationships, and nobody who will take their call. You are not most first-time founders. You arrive with something that took thirty years to build and cannot be bought at any price: a web of people who know you, trust you, and have watched your character hold up over time.

The mistake is to handle this asset carelessly, either by ignoring it out of pride or by spending it clumsily and burning through it in a single season. Both waste something genuinely rare. Here is how to use it well.

Why the network outvalues the capital

Capital is finite, and once it is spent, it is gone. A network, used with integrity, does the opposite. It compounds, because every relationship you honour tends to open two more. In practical terms, your network is the first place a new business finds the three things it struggles most to come by.

  • Your first customers. People who already trust you will try what you offer long before a stranger will, and they will forgive the rough edges of an early business, because what they are really buying is you, not a polished brand.
  • Your first honest feedback. Contacts who respect you will tell you the truth a survey never will: whether the idea holds together, where it is weak, and who else might need it.
  • Your first doors. An introduction from a trusted peer skips the long queue that swallows months of a stranger's time. In Nigerian business especially, the warm introduction is not so much a shortcut as the path itself.

How to draw on it without wearing it down

A network is a reservoir of goodwill, built up over decades. You can drain it carelessly until it runs dry, or draw on it wisely and keep it topped up. The whole difference lies in how you go about it.

Lead with reconnection, not the ask

Do not resurface after years of silence with a request already attached. Reconnect first, and mean it, before you need anything. The relationships you tended before you needed them are the ones that will still be there when you do. If your first message in five years turns out to be a pitch, you are spending goodwill you never took the trouble to restock.

Be specific, and make it easy to say yes

Vague requests are a burden. Specific ones are a gift. "Do you know anyone who might need this?" quietly asks the other person to do your thinking for you. "I am looking for procurement heads in manufacturing; does anyone come to mind?" lets them help you in about ten seconds. Respecting someone's time is itself a form of relationship-building.

Give before you take, and keep giving

The strongest networkers are, almost always, net givers. An introduction you make, a piece of insight you pass on, a door you open for someone else. These are deposits, and they have a way of coming back. Approach your network asking what you can offer at least as often as what you need, and it will grow stronger rather than thinner.

Protect the relationship above the transaction

No single deal is worth a relationship you spent decades building. If a piece of business with a contact goes wrong, protect the relationship even at the cost of the transaction. The relationship is the asset; the transaction was only ever one use of it. People who get that order the wrong way round tend to find their network quietly closing to them, and they rarely work out why.

Be honest about your new position

Some contacts will engage with you differently now that you no longer hold your old title, and that is worth meeting with grace rather than resentment. The ones who valued you, rather than your position, will still be there, and those are the relationships worth investing in. Telling one group from the other, honestly and without bitterness, is part of the transition in its own right.

Turning relationships into a business, deliberately

Leverage is not the same as exploitation. It simply means recognising that the trust you have earned is a real business asset, and stewarding it with the same care you would give to capital. So map it out deliberately: who knows you, in which sectors, with what needs, and how you might genuinely serve them. Most retiring professionals are holding a network far more valuable than they realise, for the simple reason that they have never once looked at it as an asset.

Your career built the network. The work now is to see it clearly and draw on it with integrity, so that it opens the next chapter for you rather than closing quietly behind you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Isn't it awkward to approach old contacts for business?

It is only awkward when your first contact after years of silence turns out to be a request. Reconnect genuinely before you need anything, keep your asks specific and easy to answer, and give at least as often as you take. Handled that way, drawing on your network tends to strengthen relationships rather than strain them.

What if my contacts treat me differently now that I've left my role?

Some will, and meeting that with grace rather than resentment is part of the transition. The contacts who valued you, rather than your title, will still be there, and those are the relationships worth investing in. Sorting the two honestly is useful work in itself.
