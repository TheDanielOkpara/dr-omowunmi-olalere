The 5 Questions Every Executive Must Answer Before Starting a Business

Before you register a company or spend a naira, these five questions tell a senior professional whether they are ready to start a business, and what to do if they are not yet.

By Dr. Omowunmi Olalere, Founder & CEO, Numero Group

Before you register a company or spend a single naira, there are five questions worth sitting with.

You have spent decades being decisive. You made calls that moved budgets, teams, sometimes whole divisions. So when the idea of your own business finally arrives, the instinct is to move: register the name, find premises, tell people it is happening.

Try to resist that instinct for a week.

In my experience, the most expensive mistakes are rarely made by people who lacked capital or contacts. They are made by capable, accomplished people who moved before they had answered these five questions honestly. Answer them first. If you can, you are already further along than most people who start. If you cannot, you have just saved yourself money, time, and a quiet disappointment.

1. What am I actually buying: income, occupation, or legacy?

These are three different businesses, even when they look identical on paper.

If you want income, you are building an asset that has to throw off cash reliably, and the model you choose matters far more than how much you love it. If you want occupation, something to give your days shape and your mind a challenge after a full career, then a business that gently draws down your savings while keeping you engaged might still be a sound decision, provided you have called it what it is. And if you want legacy, something to hand to a child or carry your name forward, you are making a commitment measured in years, and you should structure it that way from the very first day.

The trouble is that most people never separate the three. They chase income with a legacy mindset, or quietly fund an expensive occupation while telling themselves it is an investment. Decide which one you are really buying. Almost everything else in the plan follows from that answer.

2. How much of my capital can disappear without changing how I live?

This is the single most important number you will set, and it is the one almost nobody gives you plainly.

I call it your sleep-at-night number: the amount you could lose in full and still meet every obligation you have already made to yourself. The school fees. The medical cover. The standard of living you retired into. The support you give family. Whatever sits above that number is your true risk capital. Everything below it, your pension and the money that funds the life you worked for, stays off the table.

If you cannot say your sleep-at-night number out loud right now, you are not yet ready to commit capital. That has nothing to do with how much you have. It has everything to do with the fact that you have not yet decided what you are unwilling to lose.

3. Whose problem am I solving, and would they pay me for it today?

A good idea becomes a business the moment someone is willing to pay for it. Until then it is still only an idea.

Executives are especially exposed here, because a long career trains you to trust your own judgement. You look at a market, you see a gap, and years of pattern recognition tell you it is real. Often it is real. But a real gap and a paying customer are two very different things.

So before you build anything, find five people who match your ideal customer and ask them something specific. Not "would you use this?" Ask instead, "When did you last pay for something like this, and what did it cost you?" Their spending history will tell you what their politeness never would.

4. What will this ask of me that my career never did?

In a senior role, an entire structure worked beneath you. Someone chased the invoice. Someone fixed the printer. Someone handled the unglamorous, repetitive work that keeps an organisation moving.

In the first year of your own business, that someone is usually you.

I raise this not to discourage you but to prepare you. Are you willing to do the small, undignified work again? To sell directly? To manage cash flow week by week, instead of reading it in a quarterly report? The professionals who struggle most tend to be the ones who assumed their seniority would simply carry over. It does not carry over on its own. It has to be rebuilt in a new setting.

5. If it fails, what is my exit, and can I live with it?

Decide your stop-loss before you are emotionally invested, because once you are, you will not be able to think about it clearly.

Set it out in advance as a date, a spending limit, or a milestone. Something along the lines of: "If I have not reached this point by that date, I will close, sell, or step back, and here is what that will cost me and how I recover." Writing it down does not invite failure. What it does is take away the fear that keeps people pouring good money after bad, long past the point of sense, simply because stopping feels like an admission of defeat.

Oddly enough, a pre-decided exit is often the very thing that makes the risk worth taking at all. It is the line between a calculated decision and a gamble with your retirement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a full business plan before answering these questions?

No. Answer these five first. A business plan written before you have separated income from legacy, or named your risk capital, only documents assumptions you have not yet tested. The plan comes after the questions, not before them.

I already have capital and a strong network. Isn't that enough to start?

Capital and contacts clear two obstacles out of the way, but neither one tells you whether a paying customer exists, or whether you are ready for the operator's role. Plenty of well-funded, well-connected ventures have failed on those two points alone.
